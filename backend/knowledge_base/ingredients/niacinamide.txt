Ingredient: Niacinamide (Vitamin B3)
INCI name: Niacinamide
Also known as: Nicotinamide, Vitamin B3

Mechanism of action:
1. Inhibits melanosome transfer from melanocytes to keratinocytes → reduces hyperpigmentation
2. Upregulates ceramide, free fatty acid, and cholesterol synthesis → strengthens skin barrier
3. Modulates inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-6) → anti-inflammatory effect
4. Regulates sebum production → reduces pore appearance over time
5. Antioxidant via NADH/NADPH pathway support
6. Stimulates collagen synthesis → improves skin elasticity

Effective concentrations:
- 2-5%: Anti-inflammatory, barrier support, mild sebum regulation
- 5-10%: Measurable hyperpigmentation reduction (8-12 week timeframe in clinical trials)
- 10%: Maximum common OTC concentration — full spectrum of benefits, some users report transient flushing (rare)
- >10%: Used in some clinical trials, diminishing returns and increased sensitization risk

Monk tone specific notes:
- Tones 1-4: Primary benefit is redness reduction (post-acne erythema, rosacea), pore minimizing, and barrier support
- Tones 5-10: HIGHEST PRIORITY ingredient for hyperpigmentation and PIH. The melanosome transfer inhibition specifically targets the mechanism that creates dark marks. Combine with alpha arbutin and vitamin C for synergistic triple-mechanism brightening.
- ALL tones: SPF + niacinamide is the single most evidence-supported daily routine for skin tone evenness

Interactions and combinations:
- Vitamin C: Previously claimed to form nicotinic acid (pro-flushing) — this is a myth at room temperature. Safe to layer.
- Retinol: Synergistic — niacinamide reduces retinol irritation while both improve texture
- AHAs/BHAs: Safe to combine, niacinamide reduces post-exfoliation irritation
- No known negative interactions

Sensitization risk: Low. True niacinamide allergy is extremely rare. Transient flushing at >5% is rare but possible — occurs because mass-produced niacinamide contains trace nicotinic acid impurities.

Pregnancy safety: Safe
Photosensitivity: None
Available in: Serums, moisturizers, toners, cleansers, SPF

Key studies:
- Hakozaki et al. (2002): 5% niacinamide reduced hyperpigmentation significantly vs placebo in split-face trial
- Levin & Momin (2010): Comprehensive review of niacinamide's multiple mechanisms in dermatology
- Draelos et al. (2006): Niacinamide vs hydroquinone 4% for hyperpigmentation — comparable efficacy at 4%
