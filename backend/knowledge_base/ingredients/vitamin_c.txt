Ingredient: Vitamin C (L-Ascorbic Acid and Derivatives)
INCI name: Ascorbic Acid (pure form); multiple derivative forms below
Also known as: L-Ascorbic Acid (LAA), Vitamin C, ascorbate

Mechanism of action:
1. Antioxidant: Neutralizes ROS (reactive oxygen species) generated by UV exposure → prevents oxidative DNA damage and lipid peroxidation
2. Melanin inhibition: Inhibits tyrosinase (enzyme converting DOPA → melanin) → reduces new hyperpigmentation
3. Collagen synthesis: Essential cofactor for prolyl hydroxylase → necessary enzyme for collagen cross-linking
4. Photoprotection: Synergistic with SPF — does not replace SPF but reduces UV damage that passes through SPF
5. Reduces existing dark spots: Lightens already-formed melanin through reduction chemistry

Forms of vitamin C — ranked from strongest to gentlest:
1. L-Ascorbic Acid (LAA): Pure, most potent, requires acidic pH (pH 3.0-3.5) for stability and efficacy. Most irritating. Most prone to oxidation (turns orange/brown). 15-20% most common concentration.
2. Ascorbyl Glucoside (AA-2G): Glucose-bonded. Stable in water. Cleaved on skin by glucosidase enzymes. Gentler, stable. Effective at 10-12%.
3. Sodium Ascorbyl Phosphate (SAP): Phosphate-stabilized. Water-soluble. Gentler than LAA. 10% effective. Has anti-acne properties.
4. Magnesium Ascorbyl Phosphate (MAP): Stable, gentle, water-soluble. 5-10% effective.
5. Ascorbyl Tetraisopalmitate: Oil-soluble form — stable, penetrates well, appropriate for dry skin serums.
6. STAY-C 50 / 3-O-Ethyl Ascorbic Acid: Stable ether derivative, growing clinical evidence.

Effective concentrations:
- LAA: 10% threshold for antioxidant; 15-20% for brightening
- Derivatives: Generally 10-12% for comparable activity (converted with loss)

Stability:
- LAA oxidizes rapidly in water, light, and air → store in dark, airtight packaging
- Signs of oxidation: Yellow (slightly oxidized, still okay) → Orange → Brown (ineffective, may cause irritation)
- Derivatives (ascorbyl glucoside, SAP, MAP) are stable in water formulations for 1-2 years

Monk tone specific notes — CRITICAL:
- Tones 1-4: LAA at 15-20% is effective and generally well-tolerated. Visible brightening in 4-6 weeks. Primary application: sun protection, anti-aging, mild uneven tone.
- Tones 5-7: LAA can cause stinging at low pH (pH 3.0). The stinging itself creates micro-inflammation → which can trigger PIH — the exact opposite of the intended outcome. Use derivatives (ascorbyl glucoside, SAP) instead. Derivatives are slower-acting (8-12 weeks) but safe. Alternatively, use LAA at pH 3.5 or lower concentrations (10%).
- Tones 8-10: Strongly recommend derivatives only (ascorbyl glucoside or SAP). LAA is too high-risk for PIH. At 12% ascorbyl glucoside, clinical brightening is achievable without irritation risk. Combine with alpha arbutin and niacinamide for maximum triple-mechanism brightening.
- ALL tones: Apply AM only. Vitamin C provides antioxidant protection that pairs synergistically with SPF.

Interactions:
- Niacinamide: Safe to layer (the nicotinic acid concern is a myth at room temperature)
- SPF: Synergistic — vitamin C + SPF > either alone
- Retinol: Layer in different routines (vitamin C AM, retinol PM)
- Benzoyl peroxide: Can oxidize vitamin C — avoid same-time application

Sensitization risk: Moderate (pure LAA at high concentrations). Low (derivatives).
Pregnancy safety: Safe (vitamin C is essential for fetal development; topical application is considered safe)
Photosensitivity: None — actually PROTECTIVE against UV damage
Available in: Serums (most concentrated), toners, moisturizers, eye creams

Key studies:
- Pinnell et al. (2001): Topical LAA photoprotection study (dual antioxidant with vitamin E)
- Telang (2013): Vitamin C in skin care — comprehensive review
- Kameyama et al. (1996): Ascorbyl glucoside skin lightening clinical trial
