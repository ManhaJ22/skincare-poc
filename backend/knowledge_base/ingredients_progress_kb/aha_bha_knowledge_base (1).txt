SECTION 1: MECHANISM OF ACTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALPHA-HYDROXY ACIDS (AHAs)
AHAs are water-soluble organic acids that act primarily at the skin surface
(stratum corneum). Their primary mechanism is loosening of desmosomes — the
intercellular adhesion bonds that bind corneocytes (dead skin cells) together.
This accelerates natural exfoliation (desquamation), which averages 28 days
in young adult skin and lengthens with age or sun damage.

Secondary effects documented in the literature include:
- Stimulation of collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis in the dermis
  (Van Scott & Yu, 1974, cited in Tang & Yang, Molecules, 2018)
- Reduction in melanosome transfer between melanocytes and keratinocytes,
  contributing to improvement in hyperpigmentation
- Increased ceramide synthesis with long-term use, improving barrier function

Molecular weight (Da) determines depth of penetration. Smaller molecules
penetrate more deeply and produce stronger effects at equivalent concentration:
- Glycolic acid: 76 Da (smallest AHA; deepest surface penetration)
- Lactic acid: 90 Da (slightly larger; somewhat gentler)
- Mandelic acid: 152 Da (largest common AHA; slowest, most even penetration)

Citation: Sharad J. Glycolic acid peel therapy — a current review.
Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol. 2013;6:35-48.
Citation: Tang SC, Yang JH. Dual effects of alpha-hydroxy acids on the skin.
Molecules. 2018;23(4):863.

BETA-HYDROXY ACID (BHA) — SALICYLIC ACID
Salicylic acid (SA) is oil-soluble (lipophilic), unlike AHAs. This property
allows it to penetrate into sebaceous follicles and dissolve the sebum and
keratin plug that form comedones. Molecular weight: 138 Da.

Salicylic acid's mechanisms:
- Keratolytic: softens and loosens follicular keratin from inside the pore
- Comedolytic: breaks down both open comedones (blackheads) and closed
  comedones (whiteheads)
- Mild anti-inflammatory: inhibits prostaglandin synthesis
- Mildly antimicrobial: creates an unfavorable environment for
  Cutibacterium acnes

This makes SA uniquely suited to acne and oily-pore concerns, while AHAs work
better on surface concerns like texture, dullness, and hyperpigmentation.

Citation: Arif T. Salicylic acid as a peeling agent: a comprehensive review.
Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol. 2015;8:455-461.

SECTION 2: AHA vs. BHA — COMPARISON TABLE BY CONCERN AND SKIN TYPE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SOLUBILITY
- AHA (glycolic, lactic, mandelic): water-soluble — acts on skin surface
- BHA (salicylic): oil-soluble — penetrates sebaceous follicles

BEST TARGETS BY CONCERN
- Texture / roughness: AHA (especially glycolic acid) >>> BHA
- Surface radiance / dullness: AHA >>> BHA
- Fine lines / photoaging: AHA >>> BHA
- Hyperpigmentation / dark spots: AHA (all types) > BHA (limited evidence)
- PIH (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation): AHA (glycolic, mandelic) > BHA
- Melasma: AHA (especially glycolic) > BHA
- Pore size appearance: BHA >> AHA
- Blackheads / open comedones: BHA >> AHA
- Inflammatory acne (papules, pustules): BHA > AHA (AHA has limited benefit)
- Mixed acne with PIH: AHA + BHA combination
- Oily skin excess sebum: BHA >> AHA

BEST SKIN TYPE FIT
- Dry or dehydrated skin: Lactic acid (AHA) — also humectant properties
- Normal/combination skin: Any AHA; BHA for oily T-zone concerns
- Oily or acne-prone skin: Salicylic acid (BHA); may pair with mandelic AHA
- Sensitive skin: Mandelic acid or lactic acid (AHA); avoid high glycolic
- Darker skin tones (Monk 6-10): Mandelic acid or lactic acid preferred over
  glycolic acid; BHA at low concentrations with caution

SUN SENSITIVITY INCREASE
- AHA: Marked increase in UV sensitivity during use (see Section 7)
- BHA: Mild increase in UV sensitivity
- Both: SPF 30+ broad-spectrum sunscreen required daily during use

SECTION 3: ONSET TIMELINES BY SKIN CONCERN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NOTE: Timelines below are for OTC-strength topical products (see Section 5 for
concentrations). Professional peels produce faster results but carry higher risk.
Individual variation is significant; factors include baseline severity,
adherence, formulation pH, and skin tone.

TEXTURE IMPROVEMENT (surface smoothness, rough patches)

Mechanism: AHA desquamation removes rough, uneven stratum corneum layers.
Glycolic acid timeline (OTC 5-10%):
- Week 1-2: Increased skin shedding; some initial smoothness
- Week 3-4: First measurable texture improvement; some users notice visually
- Week 8-12: Significant improvement in photodamaged skin
Citation: Stiller MJ et al. Topical 8% glycolic acid and 8% L-lactic acid
creams for the treatment of photodamaged skin: a double-blind vehicle-controlled
clinical trial. Arch Dermatol. 1996;132(6):631-636. [22 weeks; n=74 women;
76% with GA and 71% with LA achieved ≥1-grade improvement]

Lactic acid timeline (8-12%):
- Similar to glycolic but slightly slower due to larger molecular weight
- Improvement noted from week 4-6 in texture and roughness
Citation: Stiller MJ et al. Arch Dermatol. 1996. (above; lactic acid superior
to vehicle for roughness, p<0.05, at week 22)

PORE APPEARANCE

NOTE: No acid can physically shrink pores (pore size is structural).
Improvement refers to reduced pore congestion and visibility.
Salicylic acid (BHA) timeline (1-2%):
- Week 2-4: Reduction in comedone burden and sebum within pores
- Week 4-6: First visible reduction in pore congestion appearance
- Week 8-12: Sustained improvement in pore appearance

AHAs for pore appearance: Limited efficacy. Glycolic acid may help mildly by
removing surface debris at pore openings, but BHA is significantly more effective
for this concern.

HYPERPIGMENTATION (including dark spots, uneven tone, sun-induced pigmentation)

Glycolic acid (OTC 5-10%, twice daily):
- Week 2: Early darkening reduction possible in lighter tones (Monk 1-5)
  with PIH subgroup; dark spot intensity and contrast improved from Week 2
  in Fitzpatrick IV-V subjects in one study
- Week 4: Reduction in dark spot size in Fitzpatrick IV-V subjects
- Week 8-12: 34% melanin index reduction vs. vehicle in melasma patients
  (Choi et al., 2019, cited in multiple review sources)
- Week 12: Significant improvement in mottled hyperpigmentation vs. vehicle
  in 22-week photodamage trial (lactic acid superior to vehicle p<0.05)
Citation: Stiller MJ et al. Arch Dermatol. 1996. (lactic acid superior to
vehicle for mottled hyperpigmentation at week 22)

Glycolic acid (professional peels, 35-50%):
- 12 weeks / 6 peels: 50% HASI (Hyperpigmentation Activity and Severity Index)
  decrease in serial peel study (n=25); 36% reduction in HASI vs. 24% with
  topical formula alone when combined with Kligman's formula
Citation: Sharad J. Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol. 2013. (review citing serial
peel outcomes)

Mandelic acid (OTC 10% nightly):
- Week 8: 31% reduction in hyperpigmentation scores vs. 14% with vehicle in
  30 Asian subjects (split-face study, cited in Melasyl formulation review, 2024)
- Week 12: 35% reduction in appearance of hyperpigmentation (10% serum;
  n=50; Sivasankari et al., 2013, cited in Prescription Professional NL blog;
  direct verification needed)

Lactic acid (professional peel, Jessner's formula):
- Week 12: 31% reduction in Post Acne Hyperpigmentation Index (PAHPI)
  vs. 25% for 30% salicylic acid peel
Citation: Cited in PMC11514325 (Treatment of PIH in Skin of Color, systematic
review, 2024)

ACNE (inflammatory lesions and comedones)

Salicylic acid (OTC 0.5-2%):
- Week 2-3: Purging phase possible — temporary increase in comedones as
  contents move to surface; normal if resolving within 6 weeks
- Week 4-6: First visible improvement in comedone burden
- Week 8-12: Improvement in pore appearance and reduction in inflammatory
  lesion count; 47% lesion count reduction cited (source unverified on
  page reviewed; treat as approximate)
- Week 12: Clinical improvement plateau at OTC concentrations; acne persisting
  beyond this point warrants medical review

Mandelic acid (OTC 10%) vs. salicylic acid (2%):
- Week 8: Approximately 50% reduction in inflammatory and comedonal lesions
  with 10% mandelic acid (n=60; unnamed 2009 study cited in Prescription
  Professional NL blog — verify before clinical reliance)
- Week 12: Both mandelic acid (10%) and salicylic acid (2%) reduced lesion
  counts in comparative study; mandelic acid better tolerated
  (n=45; unnamed 2015 study — verify before clinical reliance)

Glycolic acid for acne: Secondary role; limited comedolytic activity
compared to SA. May help reduce PIH following acne resolution.

DULLNESS / RADIANCE

AHAs work fastest on dullness, as the mechanism (surface desquamation) directly
removes the buildup of dead corneocytes that scatter light unevenly.

Glycolic acid (OTC 5-10%):
- Week 1-2: First radiance improvement as surface cell turnover accelerates
- Week 2-4: Noticeable improvement in skin luminosity
- Week 8-12: Sustained improvement in sallowness (yellowish dullness)
Citation: Stiller MJ et al. Arch Dermatol. 1996. (glycolic acid superior to
vehicle for sallowness, p<0.05, at week 22; lactic acid also superior for
sallowness)

Lactic acid for radiance: Similar onset to glycolic; slightly gentler and
appropriate for compromised or dry barriers.

BHA for radiance: BHA has minimal direct effect on surface dullness; its
benefit is primarily intrafollicular.

SECTION 4: RISKS FOR DARKER SKIN TONES (Monk 6-10 / Fitzpatrick IV-VI)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CRITICAL SAFETY INFORMATION FOR SKINSAGE SYSTEM

POST-INFLAMMATORY HYPERPIGMENTATION (PIH) RISK

Melanocytes in darker skin tones (Monk 6-10) are more reactive and produce
larger, more dispersed melanosomes in response to inflammation, injury, or
irritation. This is a structural biological difference, not a skin sensitivity
difference per se.

Over-exfoliation — using acids too frequently, at too-high concentrations, or
at too-low pH — constitutes a skin insult that triggers inflammatory signaling.
In Monk 6-10 skin, this inflammation can paradoxically darken the very spots
the user is trying to treat.

DOCUMENTED PIH RISK FROM CHEMICAL EXFOLIATION IN DARKER SKIN:
- In a systematic review of peel treatments for PIH in skin of color, salicylic
  acid (30%) peels in 11 patients produced adverse effects at 2 weeks including:
  itching (70%), crusting (40%), redness (40%), post-treatment hyperpigmentation
  (40%), and hypopigmentation (10%)
- Glycolic acid peels produce milder adverse effects (erythema, superficial
  desquamation, vesiculation) but are not risk-free for darker tones
- One lactic acid patient in the review developed prolonged erythema and PIH
- The systematic review states: "people with skin of color have a well-documented
  elevated risk of developing PIH transiently after peels"
Citation: PMC11514325, Treatment of Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation in Skin
of Color: A Systematic Review. J Clin Aesthet Dermatol (or similar; 2024).

An older review (2010) advised "careful selection of the peel and its use to
avoid irritation" in skin of color and noted that irritation from peels can
worsen PIH and cause new dyspigmentation, keloids, or hypertrophic scarring.
Citation: Callender VD & St Surin-Lord S. Postinflammatory Hyperpigmentation:
A Review. J Clin Aesthet Dermatol. 2010;3(7):20-31.

WARNING SIGNS OF OVER-EXFOLIATION (apply to all skin tones; higher vigilance
for Monk 6-10):
- Redness or irritation lasting more than 24 hours after use
- Unusual warmth or stinging that does not resolve within 10-15 minutes
- Visible peeling, flaking, or weeping beyond mild flakiness
- Paradoxical darkening of spots being treated
- Tight, raw, or "burning" sensation between uses

RECOMMENDED APPROACH FOR MONK 6-10 SKIN

Acid selection hierarchy (safest to least safe for PIH risk):
1. Mandelic acid (152 Da; slowest penetration, gentlest) — PREFERRED
2. Lactic acid (90 Da; moderate; also humectant) — GENERALLY SAFE
3. Glycolic acid (76 Da; most aggressive) — USE WITH CAUTION at low %
4. Salicylic acid (BHA) — use at low concentrations (0.5-1%); higher %
   carries significant PIH and adverse effect risk per clinical evidence

Concentration recommendations for Monk 6-10:
- Mandelic acid: Start at 5%; progress to 10% after 4-6 weeks tolerance
- Lactic acid: Start at 5%; progress to 8-10% after 4-6 weeks tolerance
- Glycolic acid: Start at 5% or below; maximum OTC 8-10%; avoid high-pH
  products (pH above 4.0 reduces efficacy, below 3.5 increases irritation risk)
- Salicylic acid: Start at 0.5%; maximum OTC 1%; 2% only with established
  tolerance and limited frequency (1-2x per week)

Frequency recommendations for Monk 6-10:
- Weeks 1-2: Use 2 nights per week
- Weeks 3-4: If tolerated (no irritation lasting >24h), increase to 3 nights
- Weeks 5-8: If tolerated, increase to every other night maximum
- Maintenance: 3-4 nights per week long-term
- NOTE: Never use AHA and BHA on the same night for Monk 6-10 skin until
  well-established tolerance; this doubles irritation risk

Pre-treatment recommendation (for professional peels):
- Priming the skin with a low-concentration acid for 2-4 weeks before a peel
  may reduce PIH risk by pre-adapting the skin barrier

SECTION 5: MANDELIC ACID — SPECIFIC GUIDANCE FOR DIVERSE SKIN TONES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHY MANDELIC ACID FOR DARKER SKIN TONES

Mandelic acid (alpha-phenylglycolic acid) is a 152 Da AHA derived from bitter
almonds. Its larger molecular weight compared to glycolic acid (76 Da) means
slower, more even penetration through the stratum corneum. This translates to:
- Lower peak acid concentration at any depth of the skin at any given time
- Reduced risk of triggering the inflammatory cascade that leads to PIH
- More gradual exfoliation with lower risk of over-exfoliation

It is both oil-soluble and water-soluble — making it unusual among AHAs in its
ability to penetrate into follicles to some degree, while still providing
surface-level exfoliation similar to glycolic and lactic acid.

Dermatology educators describe mandelic acid as the "preferred AHA for
Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin tones" (medspot.ai, 2024). This is widely accepted
in clinical practice, though the peer-reviewed clinical trial base is smaller
than for glycolic acid.

CLINICAL EVIDENCE (note: some studies are small and from industry-adjacent
sources; citations provided to allow direct verification)

Acne:
- 10% mandelic acid gel (8 weeks, n=60): ~50% reduction in inflammatory and
  comedonal lesions. No study identifier provided on source page; verify before
  clinical reliance.
  [Cited without DOI in: Prescription-Professional.nl, 2024]

- 10% mandelic acid solution vs. 2% salicylic acid (12 weeks, n=45): Both
  reduced lesion counts; mandelic acid better tolerated.
  [Cited without DOI in: Prescription-Professional.nl, 2024]

Hyperpigmentation:
- 10% mandelic acid serum (nightly, 12 weeks, n=50): 35% reduction in
  hyperpigmentation appearance.
  [Cited as Sivasankari et al., 2013; verify independently]

- 5% mandelic acid cream vs. 2% salicylic acid (Burns et al., 2020):
  Comparative study for hyperpigmentation.
  [Cited in Melasyl.com formulation review, 2024]

- Mandelic acid 10% nightly vs. vehicle, split-face (8 weeks, n=30 Asian
  subjects): 31% reduction in hyperpigmentation scores vs. 14% with vehicle.
  [Cited in Melasyl.com formulation review, 2024; no independent DOI available]

Melasma:
- 5% mandelic acid cream (16 weeks, n=40): 25% reduction in pigmentation.
  [Cited without DOI; study from 2017 per source; verify before clinical reliance]

Photoaging / texture:
- 15% mandelic acid peel (every 2 weeks, 12 weeks, n=30): improved texture
  and fine lines; 20% gain in elasticity. [Professional peel strength; not OTC]
  [Cited without DOI in: Prescription-Professional.nl, 2024]

MANDELIC ACID EVIDENCE CAVEAT
The clinical trial base for mandelic acid is smaller and less robust than for
glycolic acid. Many cited studies lack DOIs, control groups, or statistical
significance data in the sources available. The practical recommendation for
darker skin tones rests partly on mechanism (molecular size, penetration kinetics)
and partly on the consistently better PIH safety profile compared to glycolic
acid in clinical practice, rather than head-to-head controlled trials.

SKINSAGE RECOMMENDATION FRAMEWORK FOR MANDELIC ACID
- Monk 1-5 with sensitive skin: Good choice; gentler than glycolic
- Monk 6-10 with hyperpigmentation: First-line AHA choice; begin at 5%
- Monk 6-10 with acne: Second-line to salicylic acid; can be combined
- Monk 6-10 with both acne and PIH: Mandelic acid preferred; treats both
  without the PIH risk of glycolic acid or high-concentration salicylic

SECTION 6: CONCENTRATION GUIDELINES — OTC vs. PROFESSIONAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GLYCOLIC ACID
- OTC cosmetics (home daily use): 5-10%; pH 3.5-4.5
  - 5-7%: Entry level; twice daily; appropriate for sensitive or first-time users
  - 8-10%: Effective for texture, hyperpigmentation, and photoaging
  - Above 10%: Approaches peel territory; use with caution; not suitable for
    Monk 6-10 without established tolerance
- OTC at-home peel kits: 20-30%; use at most every 2-4 weeks; not recommended
  for Monk 6-10 without dermatologist guidance
- Professional peels: 30-70%; neutralized by aesthetician or dermatologist;
  Fitzpatrick phototype IV-VI require lower starting concentrations and careful
  monitoring
  - 20-week study used 50-68% GA peels in African American patients
Citation: Callender VD. J Clin Aesthet Dermatol. 2010.

LACTIC ACID
- OTC cosmetics: 5-12%
  - 5%: Introductory; appropriate for sensitive skin, Monk 6-10, dry skin
  - 8-10%: Effective for texture and hyperpigmentation; well tolerated
  - 12%: Upper OTC range; study-level concentration for photodamage
  - 85% (professional peel): Study used 3-minute applications monthly x3 months
    for wrinkle reduction in Fitzpatrick II-IV subjects
Citation: Prestes PS et al. Randomized clinical efficacy of superficial peeling
with 85% lactic acid versus 70% glycolic acid. An Bras Dermatol. 2013;88(1).
Citation: Stiller MJ et al. Arch Dermatol. 1996. (8% OTC cream, twice daily)

MANDELIC ACID
- OTC cosmetics: 5-15%
  - 5%: Introductory; Monk 6-10 starting concentration
  - 10%: Well-studied at-home concentration for acne and hyperpigmentation
  - 15%: Upper OTC range; also used for in-office superficial peels
- Professional peels: 15-40%; lower risk than glycolic acid peels for PIH

SALICYLIC ACID (BHA)
- FDA OTC approved for acne: Maximum 2%
  - 0.5%: Starting point; well-tolerated; suitable for sensitive or Monk 6-10
  - 1.0%: Daily use; effective for mild to moderate comedonal acne
  - 2.0%: Maximum OTC strength; spot treatment or limited-area use
- Professional peels: 10-30%
  - 20-30%: Used in clinical studies for PIH treatment in skin of color
  - Requires 2 weeks of caution post-peel due to adverse effect profile
  - FDA warns against purchasing or using high-concentration chemical peel
    products (above OTC strength) without professional supervision
Citation: FDA Drug Safety Communication. fda.gov, accessed 2024.
Citation: Arif T. Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol. 2015. (SA review; FDA approval)

PH CONSIDERATIONS
- Optimal pH for AHA activity: 3.0-4.0
- pH above 4.5: Significantly reduced free acid availability; product is less
  effective but less irritating
- pH below 3.0: Risk of significant irritation, especially for Monk 6-10 skin
- Recommendation for Monk 6-10: Look for pH 3.5-4.2 in AHA products

SECTION 7: SEVERITY MODIFIERS — MILD vs. SEVERE PRESENTATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ACNE — MILD vs. SEVERE

Mild acne (few comedones, occasional papules, no cysts):
- Salicylic acid 0.5-1% daily or BID is appropriate first-line OTC
- Improvement expected at 6-8 weeks; full response by 12 weeks
- If no improvement by 12 weeks, escalate to 2% or combination with AHA

Moderate acne (multiple comedones + papules + some pustules):
- Salicylic acid 2% daily or BID; combination with adapalene (retinoid, OTC)
- Expect partial improvement by 8 weeks; significant by 12-16 weeks
- For Monk 6-10: add mandelic acid (10%) on alternate nights to address PIH

Severe acne (nodules, cysts, widespread lesions):
- OTC acids alone are unlikely to provide adequate control
- Recommend dermatologist consultation; prescription-strength treatments needed
- Acids can be adjunctive but should not delay medical evaluation for severe cases
- Warning sign for SkinSage: "If you're experiencing cystic or nodular acne,
  over-the-counter acids can help reduce surface buildup but cannot treat the
  deep inflammation. I'd strongly recommend seeing a dermatologist."

Acne with PIH (Monk 6-10):
- PIH from resolved acne can persist months to years without treatment
- Preferred: mandelic acid (10%) + niacinamide (5-10%) for combined
  anti-acne and pigment-reducing effect
- Avoid aggressive exfoliation of active lesions — picking and over-treatment
  worsen PIH

HYPERPIGMENTATION — MILD vs. SEVERE

Mild hyperpigmentation (subtle uneven tone, light sun spots):
- OTC glycolic acid 5-8% or mandelic acid 10% (Monk 6-10)
- Improvement expected within 4-8 weeks
- Maintenance: 3-4x per week long term

Moderate hyperpigmentation (visible dark spots, post-acne marks, mild melasma):
- OTC AHA at effective concentration + vitamin C + niacinamide combination
- Expect 8-12 weeks for meaningful improvement
- Glycolic acid 50-68% peels in clinical study at 22 weeks showed significant
  improvement in African American patients; professional setting only

Severe hyperpigmentation or melasma:
- OTC acids are unlikely to produce complete resolution; set realistic expectations
- Clinical evidence: 6 peels (50-68% GA) over 20 weeks showed subjective
  improvement but no statistically significant difference vs. topical regimen
  alone on objective measures in African American patients (n=16)
Citation: Callender VD. J Clin Aesthet Dermatol. 2010.
- Recommend dermatologist consultation for prescription-grade hydroquinone (4%),
  tretinoin, or combination formulas for severe cases
- Melasma has hormonal components that topical acids alone cannot address

SECTION 8: SUN SENSITIVITY — SPF PAIRING REQUIREMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EVIDENCE

AHAs increase skin's vulnerability to UV-induced damage. The best-characterized
study used 10% glycolic acid (pH 3.5) applied daily for 4 weeks:
- Glycolic acid lowered the minimum erythema dose (MED), meaning skin burned
  at lower UV exposures
- Glycolic acid increased sunburn cell (apoptotic keratinocyte) induction by UV
- DNA damage markers (cyclobutyl pyrimidine dimers) were elevated but not
  statistically significant
- Effect was reversible: sunburn cell counts and MEDs returned to baseline
  approximately one week after stopping treatment

Citation: Perricone N & DiNardo J. Topical glycolic acid enhances photodamage
by ultraviolet light. Photodermatol Photoimmunol Photomed. 2003;19(1):21-27.
(PMID: 12713551)

FDA's position: FDA's safety review of AHAs (FDA-2018-N-3240) acknowledges the
UV-sensitizing effect and requires AHA products to include sun protection
labeling.
Reference: FDA-2018-N-3240-0063. Available: downloads.regulations.gov

CLINICAL IMPLICATION FOR SKINSAGE

This sun sensitivity effect carries a heightened implication for Monk 6-10 users
specifically:
- UV exposure while using AHAs can trigger new PIH or worsen existing spots
- The exfoliated skin surface is thinner and less able to withstand UV assault
- SPF is not optional — it is a clinical requirement for safe AHA/BHA use

SKINSAGE MESSAGING FOR SUNSCREEN PAIRING
"AHAs and BHAs remove the outer layer of dead skin cells, which also means
removing some of the skin's natural UV protection. While you are using these
ingredients, your skin is more vulnerable to sun damage. SPF 30+ broad-spectrum
sunscreen every morning — even on cloudy days — is non-negotiable when using
chemical exfoliants. For darker skin tones especially, unprotected UV exposure
while using AHAs can cause the pigment issues you're trying to fix."

SPF SELECTION FOR DIVERSE SKIN TONES
- Mineral sunscreens (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide): Leave white cast on Monk
  6-10 skin; look for micronized/nano-mineral or tinted formulations
- Chemical sunscreens (avobenzone, octinoxate): No white cast; well-tolerated
  on most skin tones
- Broad-spectrum SPF 30+: Minimum daily requirement during AHA/BHA use
- SPF 50+: Recommended if outdoors >30 minutes/day during AHA use
- Application timing: Apply sunscreen as the last skincare step in the morning

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 9: AHA / BHA COMBINATION USE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AHAs and BHAs address complementary pathways and can be used together, but
introduce additive irritation risk. For Monk 6-10 skin, same-night combination
use should be deferred until at least 8 weeks of single-acid tolerance is
established.

Safe combination approaches (once tolerance established):
1. Morning: low-concentration BHA (0.5-1% SA) for pore maintenance
   Evening: AHA (mandelic or lactic) for texture and hyperpigmentation
2. Alternating nights: AHA nights and BHA nights separated
3. Dedicated products: some formulas combine low % AHA + BHA for a gentler
   combined effect (verify pH; must be active range for both)

SECTION 10: SKINSAGE RECOMMENDATION RULES BY CONCERN AND MONK TONE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MONK TONES 1-3 (lightest)
- Higher tolerance for exfoliation but increased UV sensitivity and burning risk
- SPF pairing is critical; sun damage accumulates faster
- Glycolic acid 8-10% appropriate for texture, hyperpigmentation
- Salicylic acid 1-2% for acne and pores

MONK TONES 4-5 (medium)
- Moderate PIH risk; individual variation is high
- Glycolic acid tolerated at 5-8%; increase cautiously
- Salicylic acid 1% starting, up to 2% with tolerance
- Patch test new formulations before full-face use

MONK TONES 6-7 (medium-dark)
- Elevated PIH risk; use gentler acids preferentially
- PREFERRED: Mandelic acid (5-10%) or lactic acid (5-10%)
- Glycolic acid: Maximum 5-8% OTC; limit to 3x/week; monitor closely
- Salicylic acid: Start at 0.5%; progress to 1% if tolerated
- Never start above these concentrations without established tolerance

MONK TONES 8-10 (darkest)
- Highest PIH risk; most stringent caution required
- PREFERRED: Mandelic acid 5% (starting) to 10% (maintenance) — first-line AHA
- Lactic acid 5-8% acceptable if mandelic acid not available
- AVOID high-concentration glycolic acid OTC products
- Salicylic acid: 0.5% only initially; 1% maximum until proven tolerance
- Professional peels: Recommend dermatologist with skin-of-color expertise;
  pre-treatment priming with low-concentration acid for 2-4 weeks
