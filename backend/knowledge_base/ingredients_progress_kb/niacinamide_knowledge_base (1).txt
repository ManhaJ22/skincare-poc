================================================================================
OVERVIEW
================================================================================

Niacinamide (also called nicotinamide or vitamin B3) is a water-soluble B-vitamin
used topically in concentrations ranging from 2% to 10%. It is one of the
best-studied cosmetic actives, with consistent evidence across multiple randomized
controlled trials. It operates through several independent mechanisms, making it
one of the few single ingredients that addresses multiple skin concerns simultaneously.

It is considered safe for all skin tones (Monk 1–10), all skin types (oily,
dry, combination, normal), and all ages. It has no UV-sensitizing effect,
so it can be used in both morning and evening routines.

IMPORTANT DISCLAIMER: Niacinamide is a cosmetic ingredient, not a drug. It
addresses the appearance of skin concerns. It is not a treatment for diagnosed
medical conditions. Refer users to a board-certified dermatologist for
rosacea, melasma, eczema, or any clinically diagnosed condition.

================================================================================
SECTION 1: MECHANISMS OF ACTION
================================================================================

HYPERPIGMENTATION
Niacinamide does NOT inhibit melanin production (unlike hydroquinone or kojic acid).
Instead, it blocks the transfer of melanosomes (melanin packages) from melanocytes
to keratinocytes. This is the step that makes pigment visible in the outer skin layers.
Proposed mechanism: antagonism of PAR-2 (protease-activated receptor 2), which
mediates melanosome uptake by keratinocytes. This mechanism is downstream of melanin
synthesis, which means niacinamide will not cause paradoxical hyperpigmentation or
rebound darkening — a clinically important property for Monk 6–10 skin tones.
(Hakozaki T et al., Br J Dermatol, 2002)

BARRIER REPAIR
Niacinamide stimulates synthesis of ceramides, free fatty acids, and cholesterol in
keratinocytes — the three lipids that form the skin barrier. This reduces
transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and improves hydration directly.
(Tanno O et al., Br J Dermatol, 2000 — in vitro; Draelos Z et al., 2005 — clinical)

SEBUM REGULATION
The mechanism is not fully established. Leading hypothesis: niacinamide suppresses
sebocyte differentiation or modulates PPAR (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor)
activity in sebaceous glands. Clinical effect is a measurable reduction in sebum
excretion rate.
(Draelos Z, Matsubara D, Smiles K, J Cosmet Laser Ther, 2006)

ANTI-INFLAMMATORY / REDNESS REDUCTION
Niacinamide suppresses NF-κB signaling and reduces production of pro-inflammatory
cytokines including IL-1α, IL-6, and TNF-α in keratinocytes. This mechanistically
supports reduction in redness, blotchiness, and sensitivity.
(Bierman et al., 2020 — in vitro keratinocyte study)

TEXTURE AND PHOTOAGING
Mechanism for wrinkle/texture improvement is not definitively established. Clinical
studies show improvement in fine lines, skin elasticity, sallowness, and surface
texture at 5%, but the collagen-stimulation pathway is unconfirmed. The benefit
may be secondary to barrier improvement, reduced inflammation, and increased cell
turnover.

================================================================================
SECTION 2: EFFECTIVE CONCENTRATIONS
================================================================================

CONCENTRATION: 2%
Primary use: Sebum control, mild hydration support, entry-level sensitive skin
Evidence: Draelos et al. (2006) RCT showed ~7–10% reduction in sebum excretion
rate vs vehicle at 2%, with larger effect in the T-zone. Well-tolerated by
sensitive and reactive skin.
Best for: Oily or combination skin users who are new to actives, or who have
rosacea or reactive skin. All Monk tones.
Limitations: Minimal evidence for hyperpigmentation at this concentration.

CONCENTRATION: 3%
Primary use: Hyperpigmentation, fine lines (often combined with other actives)
Evidence: Shariff et al. (2022) split-face RCT (n=44 Chinese women, 35–60 years,
12 weeks) showed 3% niacinamide alone produced statistically significant improvement
in hyperpigmentation and fine lines vs baseline from week 4 onward. This is the
only major trial that detected significant hyperpigmentation response below 5%.
Best for: Sensitive skin users targeting pigmentation; Asian skin tones in the
Monk 4–7 range (the trial population).

CONCENTRATION: 4%
Primary use: Acne and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)
Evidence: Shalita et al. (1995) double-blind RCT (n=76, 8 weeks) found 4%
niacinamide gel equivalent to 1% clindamycin gel for reducing papules and pustules
in acne vulgaris. This is the strongest acne evidence for niacinamide.
Best for: Acne-prone skin, particularly where antibiotic resistance is a concern
or where post-acne PIH is present. All Monk tones.

CONCENTRATION: 5%
Primary use: Hyperpigmentation, photoaging, sebum, barrier, redness, texture
Evidence: This is the most studied concentration with the broadest efficacy evidence.
  - Hakozaki et al. (2002), Br J Dermatol: n=18 Japanese women, split-face RCT,
    8 weeks. Significant reduction in dyspigmentation (ITA measurement) and
    histological decrease in melanosome density in keratinocytes vs vehicle.
    68% inhibition of melanosome transfer in cell culture model.
  - Bissett et al. (2004/2005), Int J Cosmet Sci: n=50 white women with moderate
    photodamage, double-blind vehicle-controlled RCT, 12 weeks. Improvements in
    fine lines, hyperpigmented spots, red blotchiness, skin yellowing (sallowness),
    and elasticity.
  - Draelos et al. (2005): n=50 women, 12 weeks. Improved barrier function,
    consistent with ceramide upregulation.
  - Ghasemiyeh et al. (2025), RCT (n=77 completers of 99 enrolled): 3-month study
    comparing niacinamide 2–4% (in combination with tranexamic acid) vs hydroquinone
    4% in Iranian melasma patients. All arms reduced melanin index and mMASI scores
    significantly (p<0.001). Adverse reactions: 18.18% in hydroquinone arm vs ~3%
    in niacinamide arms (p=0.033). No significant difference in efficacy between arms.
Best for: All skin concerns, all Monk tones. The standard recommended concentration
for general use. The "sweet spot" with the best evidence-to-safety profile.
Clinical recommendation: Use 5% twice daily for most users.

CONCENTRATION: 10%
Primary use: Marketed for intensive treatment
Evidence: No double-blind RCT has demonstrated meaningfully superior efficacy
compared to 5% for any topical concern. The aestheticmedguide.com synthesis
(2024) states: "No evidence that >5% adds meaningfully to efficacy."
Tolerability: Stinging and irritation risk increases above 5%. One tolerability
study reported no stinging up to 10% in a controlled setting, but real-world
sensitive skin users frequently report flushing and irritation at this level.
Flushing mechanism: High concentrations of niacinamide can contain trace amounts
of nicotinic acid (niacin) as a manufacturing impurity. Nicotinic acid triggers
prostaglandin release, causing temporary facial flushing. This is not an allergic
reaction and resolves within 30–60 minutes, but can be distressing and is often
mistaken for an allergic response.
Clinical recommendation: Do NOT recommend 10% to users with:
  - Sensitive or reactive skin
  - Rosacea or erythema-prone skin
  - Any history of niacin flushing reactions
  - Monk 1–3 tones with visible facial redness
Acceptable at 10% for: oily, non-sensitive users seeking maximum sebum control
who have no adverse reaction history.

SUMMARY TABLE
Concentration | Primary Evidence | Key Benefit | Timeline

SECTION 3: ONSET TIMELINES BY SKIN CONCERN
================================================================================

The following timelines are based on twice-daily application unless otherwise noted.
"Noticeable" is defined as clinically measurable change or subjective improvement
reported by >50% of participants in cited trials.

3.1 SEBUM AND PORE SIZE REDUCTION
Timeline: 4–8 weeks
Evidence basis: Draelos et al. (2006) RCT at 2% showed measurable sebum
reduction at 8 weeks. At 5%, combined with other actives, sebum benefit may
appear earlier (4 weeks), consistent with the Shariff (2022) overall skin
texture findings from week 4.
Modifier — Skin type: Oily skin shows the most dramatic sebum response. Dry
skin may see minimal change in sebum but benefit from the hydration effect.
Modifier — Pore visibility: Pore size reduction is secondary to sebum reduction;
pores do not physically shrink but appear smaller as sebum production decreases.
Expect 4–6 weeks minimum before pore appearance change is visible.
Modifier — Concentration: 2% is sufficient for sebum control. Higher concentrations
(5%) provide faster or more pronounced effect for some users but are not required.

3.2 HYPERPIGMENTATION AND UNEVEN TONE (INCLUDING PIH AND MELASMA)
Timeline: 4–12 weeks for mild-to-moderate hyperpigmentation; 12–24 weeks for
moderate-to-severe or for deeper-toned (Monk 7–10) PIH
Evidence basis:
  - Hakozaki 2002: measurable ITA change at ~4 weeks, significant vs vehicle at 8 weeks
  - Shariff 2022: statistically significant improvement from week 4 at 3–5%
  - Bissett 2004: spot count improvements at 12 weeks
  - Ghasemiyeh 2025: melanin index reduction at 3 months
Modifier — Severity: Mild surface-level hyperpigmentation (epidermal PIH, solar
lentigines) responds faster, typically 4–8 weeks. Moderate-to-severe hyperpigmentation,
melasma, and dermal pigmentation respond more slowly and may require 16–24 weeks
of consistent use.
Modifier — Skin tone (Monk scale): See Section 4 for full detail.
Modifier — Exfoliation: Co-use of AHA/BHA can accelerate pigmentation clearance
by increasing keratinocyte turnover, surfacing new, less-pigmented cells faster.
This can shorten visible improvement to 4–6 weeks for mild cases.
Modifier — SPF: Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30+ is required for niacinamide to
address hyperpigmentation effectively. Without photoprotection, UV exposure
re-stimulates melanin transfer and neutralizes progress.

3.3 BARRIER REPAIR AND HYDRATION
Timeline: 2–4 weeks
Evidence basis: Draelos 2005 (12-week full study; barrier improvement measurable
early); atopic dermatitis patients showed improved hydration and reduced TEWL
at 4 weeks (Surjana et al., 2010, n=30).
This is the fastest-responding effect of niacinamide. Users with compromised
barriers (eczema, over-exfoliation, weather-related dryness) may notice improvement
in skin feel within 1–2 weeks, though measurable TEWL reduction takes 2–4 weeks.
Modifier — Severity of barrier damage: Acute barrier disruption (over-exfoliation,
eczema flare) responds faster. Chronic dry skin may take 4–6 weeks for stabilization.
Modifier — Concentration: 2–5% are both effective for barrier repair. No evidence
that higher concentrations improve barrier outcomes.

3.4 REDNESS REDUCTION
Timeline: 4–8 weeks for general blotchiness; individual inflammatory lesion
response may be faster (2–4 weeks)
Evidence basis: Bissett 2004/2005: significant reduction in red blotchiness at 12
weeks. Anti-inflammatory mechanism (NF-κB suppression, cytokine reduction) suggests
earlier effects on acute redness, but chronic diffuse redness takes longer.
Modifier — Rosacea: Niacinamide is frequently recommended by dermatologists for
rosacea due to its anti-inflammatory mechanism and no UV-sensitizing effect.
However, clinical trial data specific to rosacea are limited. At 2–5%, it is
considered safe for rosacea but should not be used at 10% due to potential
irritation and flushing.
Modifier — Reactive skin: Start at 2% and build up. Apply after moisturizer to
dilute concentration if initial use causes any stinging.
Modifier — Acne redness: Post-inflammatory erythema (PIE — pink/red marks after
acne) is distinct from PIH (brown marks). Niacinamide's anti-inflammatory action
addresses PIE; expect 4–8 weeks for visible reduction.

3.5 TEXTURE IMPROVEMENT
Timeline: 4–12 weeks
Evidence basis: Shariff 2022 (fine lines from week 4); Bissett 2004/2005
(texture and elasticity at 12 weeks).
"Texture" encompasses rough surface, fine lines, uneven surface feel, and dullness.
Surface roughness responds faster (4–6 weeks) due to barrier improvement and
sebum regulation. Fine lines and elasticity take longer (8–12 weeks).
Modifier — Skin type: Oily/combination skin often shows faster texture improvement
(4–8 weeks) because sebum reduction is a primary driver. Dry skin benefits more
from the barrier component, timeline similar.
Modifier — Exfoliation: AHA (glycolic, lactic acid) or BHA (salicylic acid) used
in alternation with niacinamide accelerates surface texture improvement. See
Section 5 for combination guidance.

SECTION 4: SKIN TONE CONSIDERATIONS (MONK SCALE)
================================================================================

NOTE ON EVIDENCE GAP: Most published niacinamide RCTs enrolled White (Caucasian)
women (Bissett 2004/2005) or East Asian (Japanese, Chinese) women (Hakozaki 2002,
Shariff 2022). There is a significant absence of large-scale clinical data
specifically for African, South Asian, or Afro-Caribbean populations (Monk 7–10).
The following recommendations are based on available evidence supplemented by
mechanistic rationale and dermatological consensus reviews.

MONK TONES 1–3 (LIGHTEST)
Key concerns typically: sunburn, UV damage, solar lentigines, fine lines,
redness, rosacea
Niacinamide benefits: Strong clinical evidence for hyperpigmentation (solar
lentigines) and photoaging at 5% (Bissett 2004 enrolled this demographic).
Timeline for pigmentation: 8–12 weeks for solar lentigines.
Redness/rosacea: Good tolerability. Start at 2–5%. Avoid 10% if redness-prone.
SPF note: Niacinamide does not provide SPF protection. Daily SPF 30+ is critical
for this group to prevent new UV-induced pigmentation from forming.

MONK TONES 4–6 (MEDIUM)
Key concerns typically: uneven tone, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH),
mixed UV response, early photoaging, oiliness
Niacinamide benefits: Well-supported for this range. Hakozaki 2002 enrolled
Japanese women (Monk 4–5 approximate). Shariff 2022 enrolled Chinese women (similar
range) and showed significant pigmentation benefit from week 4 at 3%.
PIH considerations: This group has elevated risk of PIH from acne, sun exposure,
and injury compared to Monk 1–3. Niacinamide's melanosome transfer inhibition
mechanism is particularly relevant because it avoids the depigmentation risk
associated with tyrosinase inhibitors.
Recommended concentration: 5% for multi-benefit use. 2–3% for hyperpigmentation
in sensitive skin.
Timeline for PIH: 8–12 weeks for mild-to-moderate epidermal PIH.
Combination: Niacinamide 5% + SPF 30+ + (optional) AHA at alternate nights
produces faster results than niacinamide alone. See Section 5.

MONK TONES 7–10 (DEEPEST)
Key concerns typically: post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH), melasma,
hyperpigmentation from any inflammatory trigger, potential keloid formation,
oiliness
Critical safety note: This group has the highest risk of PIH from any skin
inflammation, including from active ingredients that are too irritating. This
makes the choice of hyperpigmentation treatment especially important. Niacinamide
is preferred over hydroquinone, kojic acid, and glycolic acid as a first-line
option because:
  1. It does not inhibit melanin synthesis directly — no risk of paradoxical
     depigmentation (ochronosis-like reactions) seen with long-term hydroquinone use
  2. No skin bleaching — it reduces abnormal pigmentation only where melanosome
     transfer is elevated; it does not affect overall skin tone
  3. Low irritancy — irritation triggers more PIH in this group
Efficacy comparison with hydroquinone: Ghasemiyeh 2025 (melasma RCT) found
comparable melanin index reduction between niacinamide-containing formulas and
hydroquinone 4% at 3 months, with dramatically fewer adverse reactions (3% vs 18%).
Timeline for PIH in Monk 7–10: Expect 12–24 weeks for moderate-to-severe PIH,
compared to 8–12 weeks for Monk 4–6. Deeper dermal pigmentation is slower to
clear because:
  - Melanocytes are more active in response to inflammation
  - Deeper pigment deposits are not surfaced by epidermal turnover alone
  - SPF compliance has a larger impact on outcomes; re-pigmentation from UV
    exposure is a frequent cause of perceived treatment failure
Concentration for Monk 7–10: 5% twice daily is the evidence-supported standard.
Adding tranexamic acid (2–5%) has shown efficacy in combination formulas for
melasma in similar populations (Ghasemiyeh 2025). Azelaic acid (10–20% Rx or
10% OTC) is a complementary active with separate evidence for PIH in skin of color.
Redness: Erythema is less visible in Monk 7–10, but inflammation markers are
just as present. Niacinamide's anti-inflammatory mechanism is equally beneficial;
visible redness reduction may be less measurable but internal inflammation reduction
is still occurring.
Post-inflammatory erythema (PIE): PIE appears as darker brown/gray discoloration
rather than red/pink in Monk 7–10 skin, making it clinically harder to distinguish
from PIH. Niacinamide addresses both mechanisms.

SECTION 5: SEVERITY MODIFIERS FOR HYPERPIGMENTATION
================================================================================

The following tiers determine expected timeline and whether niacinamide alone
is sufficient or combination therapy is appropriate.

MILD HYPERPIGMENTATION
Definition: Small area of slightly darker tone; sun spots; faint post-acne marks
Expected timeline with niacinamide 5% + SPF: 4–8 weeks
Expected timeline without SPF: Minimal improvement likely (new UV pigmentation
replaces cleared pigment)
Recommendation: Niacinamide 5% twice daily + SPF 30+ daily. No additional
actives required.

MODERATE HYPERPIGMENTATION
Definition: Multiple spots; melasma affecting >10% of face; PIH lasting >6 months
Expected timeline with niacinamide 5% alone: 12–20 weeks
Expected timeline with niacinamide 5% + AHA/BHA (alternate evenings): 8–12 weeks
Expected timeline with niacinamide + tranexamic acid or kojic acid: 8–12 weeks
Recommendation: Combination approach with SPF. Refer to dermatologist if no
improvement at 16 weeks.

SEVERE HYPERPIGMENTATION
Definition: Deep, widespread pigmentation; persistent melasma; extensive PIH
Expected timeline with OTC actives: 24–52 weeks (1 year) for visible improvement
Recommendation: Niacinamide 5% as a maintenance and tolerability-support layer.
Strong recommendation to consult a dermatologist. Prescription options (tretinoin,
azelaic acid Rx, hydroquinone under supervision) provide faster results.
SkinSage response: Always recommend dermatologist consultation for severe
hyperpigmentation. Do not promise specific timelines.

SECTION 6: INGREDIENT INTERACTIONS
================================================================================

6.1 NIACINAMIDE + VITAMIN C (L-ASCORBIC ACID)
Myth vs evidence: A widely cited concern is that mixing niacinamide and vitamin C
produces nicotinic acid (niacin), which causes facial flushing. This fear originates
from a 1960s study that applied extreme heat and acid conditions to pure niacin (not
niacinamide) and ascorbic acid. This does not replicate skincare use conditions.
Current consensus: Safe to combine in a modern cosmetic formulation. A 2020 study
in the International Journal of Cosmetic Science found the combination safe, with
complementary barrier and pigmentation benefits.
Practical caveat: L-ascorbic acid requires a low pH (~3.5) for stability.
Niacinamide is effective at pH 4.5–7. If using separate products, apply the
vitamin C product first (low pH), allow 10–15 minutes, then apply niacinamide
at a higher pH. This preserves the efficacy of vitamin C. Combining in the same
bottle at a stable formulated pH 4.5–5.5 is an acceptable approach used by
many commercial products.
Mechanism synergy: Vitamin C inhibits tyrosinase (melanin synthesis step).
Niacinamide blocks melanosome transfer (delivery step). Combined, they target
two separate points in the pigmentation pathway — a complementary approach for
hyperpigmentation.
SkinSage recommendation: Approve combination. Advise pH-sequencing if using
separate products.

6.2 NIACINAMIDE + RETINOL / RETINOIDS
Evidence: Niacinamide's barrier-repair mechanism (ceramide synthesis, reduced TEWL)
directly addresses the primary side effects of retinol — dryness, peeling, barrier
disruption, and erythema. No RCT has tested the specific combination, but the
mechanistic rationale is strong and dermatologists widely recommend niacinamide
as a "retinoid buffer."
Shariff 2022 used niacinamide in a trial that also addressed fine lines —
the same endpoints retinol studies measure — suggesting niacinamide adds independent
texture benefit beyond tolerability support.
SkinSage recommendation: Approve and actively recommend this pairing for users
using retinol who experience irritation. Apply niacinamide in AM and retinol PM,
or apply niacinamide before retinol in a PM routine as a buffer.

6.3 NIACINAMIDE + AHA/BHA (GLYCOLIC ACID, LACTIC ACID, SALICYLIC ACID)
Interaction: No direct chemical interaction. The main consideration is pH.
AHA/BHA products work at pH 3–4. At this low pH, niacinamide may partially
hydrolyze. Applying niacinamide immediately after a low-pH acid product reduces
its local pH exposure.
Practical guidance: Apply AHA or BHA first. Wait 10–30 minutes, or use them
at different times (acid in PM, niacinamide in AM). Using niacinamide after
AHA in a layering routine without waiting is accepted by most formulators as
low-risk, as niacinamide's pH range (4.5–7) quickly restores the skin surface.
Synergy: AHA/BHA increases keratinocyte turnover, surfacing fresh unpigmented cells
faster. Niacinamide blocks ongoing melanosome delivery. This combination produces
faster visible results for PIH than either ingredient alone.
SkinSage recommendation: Approve combination with layering guidance. Recommend
using them at separate times (acid PM, niacinamide AM) for maximum efficiency
and minimum pH competition.

6.4 NIACINAMIDE + TRANEXAMIC ACID
Evidence: Ghasemiyeh 2025 (RCT, 77 completers) tested niacinamide 2–4% in
combination with tranexamic acid 2–5% vs hydroquinone 4% for melasma. The
niacinamide arms were non-inferior to hydroquinone with significantly fewer
adverse reactions (3% vs 18%). This is clinically meaningful evidence for this
combination in a hyperpigmentation-specific context.
SkinSage recommendation: Approve and highlight for melasma-focused users,
particularly Monk 6–10. Flag this combination as the evidence-based OTC
alternative to hydroquinone for melasma.

6.5 NIACINAMIDE + CERAMIDES
Mechanistic synergy: Niacinamide stimulates endogenous ceramide production.
Topical ceramides supplement the barrier directly. No RCT has tested the
combination, but the additive effect on barrier function is mechanistically sound.
SkinSage recommendation: Approve. Particularly useful for eczema-prone users
(Monk all tones), compromised barrier, or users on retinol.

6.6 NIACINAMIDE + BENZOYL PEROXIDE (BPO)
Concern: Benzoyl peroxide can oxidize and degrade niacinamide on contact,
reducing efficacy of both ingredients. This is a formulation concern (combining
in the same bottle) rather than a skincare-layering concern.
In routine layering: Apply BPO, let it absorb, then apply niacinamide separately.
No evidence of skin harm from layering, only reduced efficacy from contact
degradation if formulated together.
SkinSage recommendation: Advise separate application steps, different AM/PM timing
if possible. Flag as a "lower priority concern" — not dangerous, just reduces
niacinamide efficacy if products contact each other on the skin before absorbing.

SECTION 7: CONTRAINDICATIONS AND SENSITIVITIES
================================================================================

7.1 TRUE CONTRAINDICATIONS (RARE)
Allergy to niacinamide itself: Extremely rare. Niacinamide has one of the lowest
sensitization rates of any cosmetic active. Contact dermatitis from niacinamide
is documented but uncommon. If a user reports confirmed niacinamide allergy,
do not recommend it.

7.2 CAUTIONS (NOT CONTRAINDICATIONS)
High concentrations (10%+) in sensitive skin:
Sensitive skin, rosacea, and reactive skin users should not start at 10%.
Flushing and stinging at 10% is caused by trace nicotinic acid impurities, not
niacinamide itself. However, the user experience is uncomfortable and can be
mistaken for allergy. Recommend 2–5% for all sensitive skin types and rosacea.

Niacinamide during pregnancy:
Topical niacinamide is considered safe during pregnancy. Systemic absorption of
topically applied niacinamide is negligible. No evidence of teratogenic risk at
cosmetic concentrations. Oral niacinamide supplements at high doses (>1,500 mg/day)
carry separate considerations and are outside SkinSage's scope.

Immunosuppressed users:
Oral nicotinamide (500 mg twice daily) for skin cancer prevention showed no benefit
in transplant recipients (Allen et al., ONTRANS 2023). Topical niacinamide in
immunosuppressed users has no documented concern, but users on immunosuppressive
therapy for organ transplant or autoimmune conditions should consult their physician
before adding any active ingredient.

Existing skin cancer or pre-cancer (actinic keratoses):
Oral nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily showed 23% reduction in new non-melanoma skin
cancers (Chen et al., ONTRAC 2015). This is entirely outside SkinSage's scope
and should not be recommended or discussed as a cancer treatment or prevention tool.
Topical niacinamide in users with a skin cancer history is not contraindicated.

7.3 NOTES FOR SPECIFIC CONDITIONS
Eczema/atopic dermatitis: Niacinamide is beneficial. 5% topical niacinamide
improved hydration and reduced TEWL in atopic dermatitis at 4 weeks (Surjana et al.,
2010, n=30). Barrier repair is a core mechanism. Use 2–5%. Avoid 10% during flares.

Rosacea: Beneficial but cautious. No large RCT specific to rosacea. Anti-inflammatory
mechanism is relevant. Use 2–5% only. Avoid high concentrations (10%).
Avoid any product that causes stinging or flushing in rosacea — this indicates
an irritant response that can worsen symptoms.

Oily/acne-prone skin: 2–5% for sebum control; 4% matches clindamycin evidence for
acne lesion reduction. Safe to use alongside acne treatments. See BPO note in Section 6.

================================================================================
SECTION 8: GENERAL USE GUIDANCE
================================================================================

APPLICATION FREQUENCY: Twice daily (AM and PM) is the standard studied protocol.
Once daily is a reasonable starting point for sensitive skin before building to twice.

VEHICLE/FORMULATION: Results depend significantly on formulation, not just
concentration. pH stability (formulation around pH 4.5–7), penetration enhancers,
and product vehicle (serum vs moisturizer) affect delivery. Products formulated
below pH 3.5 may partially hydrolyze niacinamide. This is why concentration alone
does not predict outcomes. A well-formulated 5% serum outperforms a poorly
formulated 10%.

SUNSCREEN IS NON-OPTIONAL: For any hyperpigmentation or photoaging goal, SPF 30+
broad-spectrum daily sunscreen must be part of the routine. Niacinamide clears
existing pigment transfer, but UV exposure continuously stimulates new pigmentation.
Without SPF, niacinamide's effects on hyperpigmentation are significantly diminished.
SPF formulation note for Monk 7–10: Mineral (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) sunscreens
in standard white formulations leave visible white cast on darker skin tones. For
Monk 6–10, recommend iron-oxide-tinted mineral sunscreens or modern transparent
chemical sunscreens. See separate SPF knowledge base document.

TIMELINE SETTING WITH USERS: Be direct. Use these ranges:
  - "You may feel skin smoother and more hydrated in 2–4 weeks."
  - "Oiliness reduction typically appears in 4–8 weeks."
  - "Dark spots and uneven tone take 8–12 weeks minimum. If your spots are deep
     or extensive, 16–24 weeks is realistic."
  - "If you see no change at 12 weeks, we should look at adding a complementary
     ingredient or recommend a dermatologist visit."
